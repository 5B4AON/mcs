2.1  How the Decoder Works

The decoder listens for key-down / key-up timing events from every enabled input source and converts the resulting dit-dah patterns into text. Decoded characters appear in the Morse Decoder panel.

Each incoming interval (key-press duration or silence gap) is measured in milliseconds and classified as a dit, dah, intra-character gap, inter-character gap or word space using adaptive timing thresholds.

2.2  Dual Calibration — RX and TX Pools

The decoder maintains two independent calibration pools:

PoolPurposeColour Tag
RX (Receive) Incoming Morse from another operator — CW tone detector, MIDI input, serial input, or pilot-tone mic. RX text colour (green by default)
TX (Transmit) Morse you are sending — keyboard, mouse or touch keyer input. TX text colour (yellow by default)

Each pool tracks its own rolling average of dit/dah durations and derives its own WPM estimate independently. This is essential because the person you are working will almost certainly send at a different speed to you. If both speeds were mixed into a single pool, the adaptive thresholds would drift and produce decoding errors.

The main screen shows four read-only WPM indicators: RX, TX, Keyer and Encoder. The kebab menu (⋮) contains interactive +/− buttons for each, and Reset RX Cal / Reset TX Cal buttons to clear the accumulated calibration samples and restart auto-calibration from the current WPM setting.

2.3  Auto-Calibration

When a human operates a key (straight key or hand-sent paddle), element durations are never mathematically perfect. The auto-calibration algorithm measures each interval and, by dividing by the expected unit count (1 for dit, 3 for dah, etc.), estimates the current dit-unit length. A rolling average of these estimates converges on the sender's actual speed within a few characters.

Tip: Set the initial Decoder WPM reasonably close to the expected speed. The auto-calibrator works faster when it starts near the right value.

2.4  Perfect Timing (Keyer-Generated Elements)

When you use any of the iambic paddle modes (Iambic A, Iambic B, Ultimatic or Single Lever), the keyer generates dits and dahs with mathematically exact durations derived from the Keyer WPM setting. These perfect-timing elements must not be fed into the auto-calibration pool because their artificially precise durations would skew the adaptive averages that are meant to track natural human variation.

The application handles this automatically: when the iambic keyer produces an element, it sets a perfect-timing flag. The decoder then uses a fixed threshold calculated from the Keyer WPM instead of the adaptive threshold, and does not record any calibration samples. You do not need to configure anything — it just works.

Note: Straight-key input is always human-timed, even when sent from the keyboard, mouse or touch keyer. Straight-key events do feed into the calibration pool assigned to that input.

2.5  Full-screen conversation

Click the ⛶ button in the main toolbar and choose Full-screen conversation to open one large view for the whole exchange. RX and TX text appear in different colours so you can follow the conversation at a glance.

The full-screen conversation has its own independent text buffer. Clearing text in the fullscreen view does not affect the main decoder panel, and vice versa. Both buffers accumulate text continuously and survive closing and reopening the fullscreen modal — your conversation history is preserved until you explicitly clear the buffer.

Pressing the Clear button (🗑) opens a small context menu with two options: This text area clears only the full-screen conversation, while All text areas clears the main output and full-screen conversation. On the main screen, the encoder’s clear menu additionally offers an Input box option that empties the text-entry field without touching any conversation log.

2.6  Lens mode

Lens is a teaching aid. Open the fullscreen kebab menu (⋮) and choose Lens. While it is on, the whole screen shows only the latest character, large and centred, with its dots and dashes underneath. It works in both Key and Type mode, and for both RX and TX.

  • The dots and dashes appear as each one is keyed or played and stay until the next character starts. A keyed character is shown once the decoder has recognised it, so you can watch the symbols first and then see the letter.
  • Typed text follows the sound: each letter appears as it starts playing. In Send on Enter mode nothing appears until you press Enter.
  • The input’s name, if it has one, is shown at the top left in small letters. The RX/TX colours (or the input’s own colour) apply; the font size, bold and row spacing settings do not.
  • The view fits the window. When a phone keyboard is open the view shrinks to the space that is left, and it keeps clear of the touch keyer.
  • Spaces and word gaps do not clear the screen. Clear does.
  • Lens and the Key / Type choice are remembered: the fullscreen view opens the way you left it (the defaults are Lens off and Key). Both are saved with your settings and are included in Presets, so a preset can switch a classroom into Lens at once. During Copy Practice in Type mode the normal practice view is shown; text blur hides only the big letter and the hold-to-reveal button still works.
  • Choose a Font in the fullscreen menu. Five faces are offered, picked for reading single characters: Typewriter (Courier), Classic (Times), Clean (Arial), Wide (Verdana) and Code (Consolas, with a distinct zero; Typewriter is the default). The font applies to the whole fullscreen view, not only Lens, and is saved with your settings and included in Presets, together with Lens, Key / Type, the font size and the colours.
  • On a phone or tablet the letter and symbols are re-fitted into the space above the on-screen keyboard whenever it opens or closes.
